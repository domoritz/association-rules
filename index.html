What are Association Rules?

Imagine you're a store manager looking at thousands of customer receipts. You notice that customers who buy bread and butter often buy milk too. This insight could help you arrange your store layout or create targeted promotions.

Association rule mining is a technique that automatically discovers these interesting relationships in large datasets. The 1993 paper by Agrawal, Imieliński, and Swami introduced this powerful concept along with an efficient algorithm to find these rules.

The Classic Example: "Customers who purchase diapers also tend to purchase beer" — a famous (possibly apocryphal) discovery that illustrates the surprising insights association rules can reveal.

Key Concepts

Itemsets and Rules

An itemset is simply a collection of items that appear together in transactions. For example, {Bread, Milk} is a 2-itemset.

An association rule has the form X → Y, meaning "if a customer buys items in X, they are likely to also buy items in Y." For example: {Bread, Butter} → {Milk}.

Measuring Interestingness

The paper introduced two fundamental measures to evaluate rules:

Support

support(X) = count(X) / N

How frequently an itemset appears in the database. High support means the pattern is common.

Confidence

confidence(X→Y) = support(X∪Y) / support(X)

How often the rule is true. If confidence is 80%, then 80% of transactions containing X also contain Y.

Lift

lift(X→Y) = confidence(X→Y) / support(Y)

How much more likely Y is given X, compared to Y alone. Lift > 1 indicates a positive association.

The Apriori Algorithm

Finding all association rules efficiently is challenging because the number of possible itemsets grows exponentially. The paper's key insight is the Apriori principle:

Apriori Principle: If an itemset is infrequent, all its supersets must also be infrequent. Conversely, all subsets of a frequent itemset must be frequent.

This allows us to prune the search space dramatically by eliminating candidates that can't possibly be frequent.

Find Frequent 1-Itemsets

Count the support of each individual item. Keep only those meeting the minimum support threshold.

Generate Candidates

Combine frequent (k-1)-itemsets to create candidate k-itemsets. Prune any candidate with an infrequent subset.

Count Support

Scan the database to count support for each remaining candidate.

Repeat

Continue until no more frequent itemsets can be found, then generate rules from all frequent itemsets.

Real-World Applications

🛒 Market Basket Analysis

The original motivation: understanding what products customers buy together to optimize store layouts, cross-selling strategies, and inventory management.

🎬 Recommendation Systems

Netflix, Amazon, and Spotify use variants of association rules to suggest "customers who liked X also liked Y."

🏥 Medical Diagnosis

Finding associations between symptoms and diseases, or between medications and side effects.

🔒 Fraud Detection

Identifying unusual patterns of transactions that deviate from normal association rules.

🌐 Web Usage Mining

Understanding user navigation patterns to improve website design and personalization.

Summary

The 1993 paper made three major contributions:

  1. Formalized the problem of mining association rules with support and confidence measures
  2. Introduced the Apriori principle for efficiently pruning the search space
  3. Provided practical algorithms that could handle large databases

This foundational work spawned an entire field of research and remains one of the most cited papers in data mining. The concepts of support, confidence, and the Apriori algorithm are now standard tools in every data scientist's toolkit.
